2. Which organ, tumor types, and surrounding tissues are targeted? Why it matters: if these differ from kidney samples, the published model cannot establish applicability.
3. How does your setup differ in wavelengths, detector, window, and tissue contact? Why it matters: if geometry changes, collect new calibration and validation data.
4. How will histopathology identify the tissue actually measured? Why it matters: if locations cannot be matched, boundary labels may be unreliable.
5. What performance and workflow requirements must be met? Why it matters: if missed tumors dominate the risk, overall accuracy alone cannot establish acceptance.

### Recommendation
Use the paper as evidence motivating development, with its experimental scope stated explicitly. It investigated frozen/thawed, excised kidney biopsies after nephrectomy—not live surgical boundaries. [S03 p.3](/materials/S03_LED_Kidney_Study_2017.pdf#page=3)

Appropriate wording: “The study demonstrated proof-of-concept discrimination of excised kidney tissues using LED-based NIR sensing; surgical readiness remains unestablished.” [S03 p.14](/materials/S03_LED_Kidney_Study_2017.pdf#page=14)

Test NIR alone first. The paper identifies water, lipid, and scattering information as the physical basis for discrimination. [S03 p.7–8](/materials/S03_LED_Kidney_Study_2017.pdf#page=7)

### Measurement concept
Compare full-spectrum diffuse-reflectance NIR with the proposed LED sensor at registered tissue locations. The study used diffuse reflectance with controlled probe positioning. [S03 p.4](/materials/S03_LED_Kidney_Study_2017.pdf#page=4)

As a proposed experiment, vary contact and positioning deliberately rather than assuming the published geometry transfers. Confirm the window, fiber coupling, and surgical interface with AFS.

### Spectral range
The prototype LED maxima were 0.94, 1.17, 1.30, and 1.44 µm; these are study inputs, not established optimal wavelengths for your use. [S03 p.5](/materials/S03_LED_Kidney_Study_2017.pdf#page=5)

The listed VIS-NIR silica fiber family covers these wavelengths; this does not verify your assembled probe. [S02 p.6](/materials/S02_AFS_Process_Control_Slides.pdf#page=6)

### Risks and uncertainty
- Frozen/thawed specimens do not establish performance on living tissue. [S03 p.3](/materials/S03_LED_Kidney_Study_2017.pdf#page=3)
- Reported refined-model results excluded a tumor sample and selected measurement locations; quoting them alone would conceal failures. [S03 p.12](/materials/S03_LED_Kidney_Study_2017.pdf#page=12)
- Scattering carried useful discrimination information; scatter correction actually degraded the study’s sensor models. [S03 p.11](/materials/S03_LED_Kidney_Study_2017.pdf#page=11)
- In general, blood, pressure, motion, and surface changes could mimic tissue differences; test these rather than assuming robustness.
- AFS must confirm hardware suitability; clinical collaborators must establish the clinical validation pathway.

### Feasibility plan
1. Lab screening: measure representative client tissue with full-spectrum NIR and the proposed sensor, including boundary regions and physical variation.
2. Reference comparison: obtain spatially matched histopathology. As proposed validation safeguards, hold out entire patients and report all exclusions and classification failures.
3. In-line trial: adapt this stage to prospective intraoperative research, initially without sensor-driven surgical decisions, under an agreed clinical protocol.
4. Scale-up: proceed only after independent validation; establish setup-change controls, model maintenance, and revalidation procedures.

**Decision basis:** agree clinical go/no-go criteria before testing; the published findings alone cannot satisfy them.
